one flow.
one request.
one verdict.

An intrusion detection system that shows TypeSafe's Jev labeled flows it has already seen, asks two typed questions about the next flow and joins the answer with a nearest-neighbour vote, with no text to parse.

Tested on two public datasets of network attacks, CIC-IDS2017 and TRUSTLab, against a Random Forest, two kNN, an Isolation Forest and Claude Sonnet 5.5. The 2,000 test flows of each dataset are judged in the order they ended, and the true label of each one comes back 200 flows later.

Each F1 is read at one alarm cut per dataset, calibrated before the test and shared by every detector, over the three seeds of JevIDS, and covers only the flows judged before any label of their attack came back. The ROC-AUC, which needs no cut, is 0.994 to 0.995 on CIC-IDS2017 and 0.930 to 0.933 on TRUSTLab, the highest of the detectors tested. On TRUSTLab the bootstrap range of the F1 difference between JevIDS and the Random Forest contains 0.

the problem

A flow is the summary of one conversation between two computers, such as its packets, bytes, duration and port. An intrusion detection system (IDS) judges each flow as benign or attack. Two facts make this hard. Some attacks are new to the system, and the right answer arrives late, because an analyst confirms an attack only after someone investigates it.

zero-day attack. Here, a flow whose attack name never appears in the system's starting memory. A flow is at first contact when it is judged before any flow of its attack name has come back with its label. The results count these flows.

The delay D is counted in flows. With D = 200, a label comes back after 200 more flows, longer than every zero-day burst in the CIC-IDS2017 test.

how it works

JevIDS runs two detectors over the same stream of flows and joins their answers. The first is Jev, a System One Model from TypeSafe. It reads a block of text with the categories, k labeled flows per category (the examples) and the flow to judge, and answers typed questions with probabilities, so there is no text to parse.

  1. input

    one flow

    The record of a single connection, 78 features, plus the examples the Selector chose.

  2. jev

    one request

    State: the instructions, the categories, the examples and the flow. Questions: is_attack and category.

  3. output

    one answer

    attack · p_attack 0.81
    DoS · confidence 0.92

The KController sets k per category, starting at 1, raising it when Jev names the wrong category and lowering it when Jev is right. The Selector picks which examples, preferring flows close to the one under test, recent ones and ones Jev doubted. The matched kNN lets as many nearest flows vote as the Selector gives Jev at k = 4, at no cost. The full JevIDS turns each answer into its percentile on a development stream, because the two detectors catch different attacks, and raises the alarm when their mean reaches the cut, 0.2608 on CIC-IDS2017 and 0.377 on TRUSTLab.

The dashed line carries each label back to the memory D flows later.

how it was tested

Both datasets are public lab traffic. Each attack name's flows of each day were cut in half in time, the earlier half feeding the pool, the memory at the start of the test, and the later half the test. CIC-IDS2017's three zero-day names leave the pool while their categories keep other names. TRUSTLab's five zero-day families leave it whole, so they have no example until their first labels come back.

the two datasets · 2,000 test flows each
dataset captured pool benign zero-day at first contact zero-day attack names
CIC-IDS2017, relabelled by Liu et al. (IEEE CNS 2022) July 2017 88,066 1,046 309 SSH-Patator, DoS Slowhttptest, Infiltration Portscan
TRUSTLab, Cano Baños and Villafranca Albaladejo (Frontiers in Computer Science, 2026) May and June 2025 150,000 798 338 C2Beaconing, DDoS, DoS, MITM, WebBased

The rivals are a Random Forest, an Isolation Forest trained on benign traffic, a kNN with 4 neighbours and the matched kNN, each refitted at every step on the memory JevIDS sees, and Claude Sonnet 5.5 with the same Selector and KController. Jev alone, the full JevIDS and Sonnet ran with three seeds, the classical detectors once.

The ROC-AUC, the primary metric, is the chance that a detector scores a random zero-day flow above a random benign one, 0.5 for a random score. The PR-AUC, the secondary one, weighs each false alarm by how much it dilutes the share of alarms that are real attacks. Neither needs a cut. The F1 reads every detector at one alarm cut per dataset, calibrated before the test. A bootstrap over 2,000 redraws gives 95 % ranges, and McNemar's exact test compares the flows two detectors disagree on.

The full JevIDS has the highest ROC-AUC on both datasets. Its PR-AUC is the highest on TRUSTLab, 0.908 to 0.911 against 0.796 for the Random Forest, and on CIC-IDS2017 it ties with Sonnet's, with the interval of the difference containing 0 in every seed. On CIC-IDS2017 Jev alone ranks the flows almost as well, yet at the cut it flags every zero-day flow along with 121 to 129 benign ones, and the full JevIDS catches 288 to 291 with 15 to 18 false alarms. On TRUSTLab the full JevIDS and the Random Forest reach a similar F1, and the bootstrap interval of the difference contains 0 in every seed.

Flow by flow, McNemar's exact test finds the full JevIDS catching more zero-day flows than the Random Forest on TRUSTLab in every seed (10 to 2 discordant flows in seed 0, p = 0.039), and its ROC-AUC lies above the Random Forest's in every seed. The three seeds judge the same flows, so they are not independent evidence.

Sonnet ties the full JevIDS on CIC-IDS2017, with an F1 of 0.932 to 0.939 against 0.937 to 0.946, and the interval of the difference contains 0 in every seed. On TRUSTLab the full JevIDS leads in F1, in ROC-AUC and in PR-AUC in every seed, with an F1 of 0.837 to 0.840 against 0.706 to 0.726. Sonnet also refused 9 to 16 calls per run on CIC-IDS2017 and 72 to 79 on TRUSTLab, judging them cyber content.

what each part adds

Each part of Jev was removed or swapped in turn, and the full JevIDS was rebuilt on each run, at D = 200 with seed 0.

The Selector matters most. Without it, the ROC-AUC falls from 0.995 to 0.951 on CIC-IDS2017 and from 0.930 to 0.875 on TRUSTLab, the PR-AUC from 0.976 to 0.844 and from 0.908 to 0.836, and the false alarms rise from 15 to 188 and from 28 to 223. A fixed k = 1 ties with the KController in F1 on both datasets, and its ROC-AUC on TRUSTLab falls to 0.893. Its run there costs 0.39 USD against 1.32, with no failed call against 144, because the KController's larger requests outgrow TypeSafe's input limit. Seeds 1 and 2 of the fixed-k lines agree.

what it costs

On CIC-IDS2017 Jev read about 3,000 tokens per flow, so a run of 2,000 flows cost 0.25 USD at TypeSafe's list price (127 USD per million flows), with a median call of 0.23 s. On TRUSTLab, with sixteen categories, it read about 15,500 tokens per flow, 1.30 to 1.32 USD per run, with a median call of 0.29 to 0.30 s. The matched kNN adds no bill. Sonnet cost 15.76 to 15.82 USD per run on CIC-IDS2017 and 51.88 to 52.27 USD on TRUSTLab, 62 and 39 to 40 times what Jev cost, and its median call took 1.82 to 1.92 s.

limits worth knowing

  • On TRUSTLab, 144 to 155 calls per seed failed near the end of the stream, because ten categories had reached k = 8 and the request outgrew TypeSafe's input limit. None of them fell on a zero-day flow at first contact, and the full JevIDS falls back on the kNN for them.
  • The F1 peak on CIC-IDS2017 is narrow. In seed 0 the full JevIDS's F1 is 0.848 at a cut of 0.25, 0.946 at 0.2608 and 0.877 at 0.27, so the F1 depends on a cut taken from another stream.
  • Every label comes back, missed attacks included. A real security team confirms only some of them.
  • Jev's cost is TypeSafe's list price, which may differ from the bill.

try it

git clone https://github.com/jev-sec/jev-ids.git
cd jev-ids
uv sync
.env holds TYPESAFE_API_KEY for Jev and ANTHROPIC_API_KEY for the Sonnet 5.5 baseline.

Download CICIDS2017_improved.zip from the relabelled version of Liu et al. into data/raw/cicids2017/, or the TRUSTLab files, and prepare them once. The test splits are committed, so only the pool needs the raw data. 

uv run python -m scripts.prepare_cicids2017
uv run python -m scripts.prepare_trustlab --raw <folder holding the 16 *.csv.gz>
uv run jev-ids run --dataset data/cicids2017/dataset.json --detector jev --split paper --delay 200 --k 1 --control aimd --selection score+nearest --seeds 0

The run writes results/<timestamp>-cicids2017-jev-paper/ with config.json, one JSON row per flow in predictions.jsonl and Jev's raw answers in responses.jsonl. The README shows how to run the baselines and reproduce every number on this page.

bring your own flows

JevIDS is an independent research prototype, and its numbers belong to these two datasets. Beside a commercial IDS it sits off the packet path, fed from the flow exporter through a queue, because a call takes about a quarter of a second and the API is rate-limited.

To try it on your flows, copy a dataset card such as data/cicids2017/dataset.json and the request prompts/cicids2017/jev.json, seed the memory with one labeled flow per category and measure on a labeled split of your own before trusting any number. Only flow features leave your network, never payloads, and every request goes to TypeSafe's API.
